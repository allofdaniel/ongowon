Minhwa Wish Guide

Korean minhwa symbols and the wishes they carry

Minhwa are Korean folk paintings that people in the late Joseon era hung at home to wish for good fortune. Each animal, flower and object in them had a set meaning. Here are the meanings behind the wishes Ongowon puts on its blankets, with sources.

Carp

Wish
Success in exams and career
Painting type
Yakrido (躍鯉圖), the leaping carp

A carp leaping through the waves under the morning sun. It comes from the 'Dragon Gate' legend, in which a fish that swims up the rapids of the Dragon Gate on the upper Yellow River becomes a dragon — a symbol of overcoming a hard threshold and rising in the world.

Crane & pine · the Ten Longevity Symbols

Wish
Health and long life
Painting type
Sipjangsaengdo (十長生圖)

A painting gathering ten things believed to live long: the sun, clouds, mountains, water, rocks, cranes, deer, turtles, pine trees and the mushroom of immortality. It carries the wish to live long without growing old. The crane and the pine are both among the ten.

Sources

Peony

Wish
Wealth and prosperity
Painting type
Morando (牡丹圖)

Peony paintings are classed as gilsangdo — auspicious pictures that express a wish for wealth and happiness through objects. For their large, lavish blooms, peonies were also favoured by the Joseon royal court as a symbol of wealth and abundance.

Note · The 'symbol of wealth and abundance' description is based on a news article about the National Palace Museum's 'Hello, Peony' exhibition.

Chaekgado (bookshelf painting)

Wish
Learning and skill
Painting type
Chaekgado (冊架圖) · Chaekgeori

Shelves filled with books, writing tools and ceramics. It began in the late 18th century with King Jeongjo's vision of governing through books, became a symbol of valuing learning (文), and by the 19th century had spread to ordinary people as a central theme of minhwa.

Note · The direct link to wishes for study and academic success comes from an exhibition article, not from the encyclopedia.

Magpie & tiger

Wish
Warding off misfortune
Painting type
Jakhodo (鵲虎圖)

A painting of a tiger with a magpie. Surviving magpie-and-tiger paintings bear the inscription 'the tiger drives away the three calamities, the dragon brings the five blessings' (虎逐三災 龍輸五福), showing they were used to keep bad luck away.

Note · The same encyclopedia's 'Jakhodo' entry also mentions a satirical reading: the tiger as the powerful and the magpie as the common people.

Sources

Bat

Wish
Good fortune
Painting type
Pyeonbokmun (蝙蝠文), bat motif

In Chinese characters a bat is written 'pyeonbok' (蝙蝠). Its second syllable, bok (蝠), sounds the same as bok (福), 'good fortune', so the bat became a symbol of luck. Bats were painted onto fabric, furniture, bowls and rice-cake stamps in place of the character 福, and five bats stood for the Five Blessings.
